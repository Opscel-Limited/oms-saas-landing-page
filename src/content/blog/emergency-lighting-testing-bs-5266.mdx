Emergency lighting testing follows a simple rhythm: a **short functional test every month**, and a **full rated-duration test once a year**, usually three hours. The code of practice that sets this out is BS 5266-1, revised in 2025, used alongside BS EN 50172. The person responsible for the premises has to make sure the tests happen and are recorded. This guide covers what each test involves, who can carry it out, and what should go in the logbook.

## How often should emergency lighting be tested?

| Test | How often | What it checks | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functional test | Monthly | Each emergency light and exit sign comes on when the mains supply fails | Short: just long enough to confirm every fitting works |
| Full-duration test | Annually | The batteries last for the system's full rated duration | The rated duration, usually 3 hours |

Both tests should be recorded every time, including when everything passes.

## Is emergency lighting testing a legal requirement?

The duty is in law; the method is in the standard. In England and Wales, the **Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005** requires the Responsible Person to make sure emergency routes and exits are lit where needed, and that fire safety equipment is kept in efficient working order and good repair. Scotland and Northern Ireland have equivalent duties.

**BS 5266-1** is the code of practice for emergency lighting of premises. It isn't law, but it is what fire risk assessors, enforcing authorities and insurers measure a system against. Your [fire risk assessment](/blog/fire-risk-assessment-uk) should identify where emergency lighting is needed, and the testing keeps it working.

## What changed with BS 5266-1:2025?

BS 5266-1:2025 replaced the 2016 edition in late October 2025. The main changes reported by BSI and trade bodies:

- **Wider scope.** It now covers local area lighting and standby lighting as well as emergency escape lighting.
- **Aligned with European standards.** It is used alongside **BS EN 50172:2024** and **BS EN 1838:2024**.
- **Private dwellings excluded.** It doesn't apply to individual homes, but it does cover the common access routes in blocks of flats and maisonettes.

If your logbook or test forms still cite BS 5266-1:2016, update them.

## What happens in a monthly test?

The monthly test is a short functional check that simulates a mains failure:

1. Switch the emergency lighting circuit to battery, usually with the secure test key (often called a "fish key") at the test switch.
2. Walk the route and check that **every** emergency luminaire and exit sign lights up.
3. Restore the mains supply and check that charging indicators come back on.
4. Record the result, including any fitting that failed.

Keep the test short. It only needs to prove each fitting works, not drain the batteries.

## What happens in the annual full-duration test?

Once a year, the system is run on battery for its **full rated duration**, usually three hours. The normal lighting circuit is switched off, and every fitting should stay lit for the whole period. Any fitting that dims or goes out before the end has failed, usually because of a battery near the end of its life.

After a full-duration test, the batteries are flat and need time to recharge. Until they have, the building has reduced emergency lighting cover. Plan the test for a time when the premises is empty or low-risk, and don't schedule it just before the building is occupied overnight.

## Who can carry out emergency lighting tests?

The Responsible Person must make sure the tests are done, but they don't have to do them personally. The tests should be carried out by a **competent person**:

- **Monthly tests** are often done by trained site staff, such as a facilities manager or caretaker, using the test key.
- **Annual full-duration tests** are usually done by a competent contractor. The FPA notes they're often carried out during a fire alarm service visit.

Systems with **automatic testing** (covered by BS EN 62034) run the tests themselves and report faults. Someone still has to check the results, act on failures and keep the records.

## What should go in the emergency lighting logbook?

Every fitting should have a location reference so failures can be traced. For each test, record:

- the date and the type of test (monthly or annual)
- the result for each fitting, and any that failed
- what was done to fix each failure, and when
- who carried out the test

Keep the logbook on site. A fire risk assessor or enforcing officer will ask to see it.
