When your job software is down, the first job is to keep engineers working and record what they do, not to fix the software. Write down what happens on site, tell customers early and honestly, and export what you can. Everything else can wait until the system is back.

On 21 October 2026 the PHP project released emergency patches for two critical vulnerabilities that could let an attacker run code on a web server without logging in ([PHP security advisory](https://www.php.net/archive/2026.php#2026-10-21-5)). The National Cyber Security Centre told organisations to update PHP and to identify which of their systems run it ([NCSC alert](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/news/critical-security-issue-affecting-php)). Some organisations took sites and services offline while they patched.

Whatever caused your outage, the questions are the same. This plan works for a patching window, a supplier fault or a dead broadband line.

## What should you do in the first hour?

Work through these in order:

1. **Check it is the software, not you.** Try another device and another network, then check your supplier's status page or social accounts.
2. **Find out who is affected.** Is it the office only, or are engineers locked out too?
3. **Get engineers what they need.** Call or message each one with the address, contact name, access notes and scope for today's jobs.
4. **Capture the schedule.** Take screenshots of today's and tomorrow's jobs from wherever you can still see them: your calendar, confirmation emails, your phone.
5. **Pick two people.** One talks to customers and one talks to the supplier, so nobody repeats the same call.

## How do engineers keep working without the system?

An engineer who knows the job can usually carry on. What changes is where the record goes. Put a simple fallback kit in every van before you need it:

| Item | What it is for |
|---|---|
| Paper job sheets | What was done, times on site, parts used, customer sign-off |
| Blank certificate forms | Your certification scheme's forms or the BS 7671 model forms, completed formally once systems are back |
| Test result sheets | Readings written down as they are taken, such as Zs and insulation resistance |
| Defect log | Observations, locations and what the customer was told |
| Phone photos | Date-stamped evidence of the installation, the defect and the finished work |

The formal certificate or report is the record that counts, but the readings and notes you take on site are what it is built from. Write them down at the time, not from memory at the end of the day.

## What should you record while the system is down?

Record what you would normally enter in the system or the site logbook:

- The date, site and who attended.
- What you inspected or tested, and the results.
- Any defects, with their location and your classification.
- What you told the customer, and when.

Anything dangerous should not wait for the software. If an electrical job turns up a C1 or C2, or a fire alarm fault leaves a system impaired, tell the customer on the day and confirm it in writing.

## What should you tell customers?

Keep it short and honest: what is affected, what it means for them and when you will update them next.

- **Do** say which appointments or documents are affected, and give a time for your next update.
- **Don't** promise a restoration time you do not control.
- **Don't** blame a named supplier unless the supplier has confirmed the cause itself.
- **Don't** send half-finished certificates or invoices to fill the gap. A late, correct document is better than a quick wrong one.

## What should you export today?

If you can still log in, export in this order: customers and sites, open and upcoming jobs, outstanding quotes and invoices, then certificate PDFs. Our guide on [what to export before switching job software](/blog/export-data-before-switching-job-software-uk) has the full checklist.

If you cannot log in, much of the picture sits elsewhere. Your accounting software holds invoices and customer details, your email holds quotes and confirmations, and your phone holds photos.

Exports contain personal data. Keep them somewhere secure and delete them when you no longer need them.

## What should you ask your software supplier?

Whether you stay or move later, an outage is the moment to ask:

- What happened, and where will you post updates?
- How often is our data backed up, and how would you restore it?
- Can I export my customers, jobs and certificates myself, and in what formats?
- What do my engineers' phones do when the connection drops?
- What is your support route during an incident, and what does my contract promise?

## How does the Opscel field app behave when the connection drops?

Opscel's app and website are not built on PHP, so this particular flaw is not one we have to patch. That is not a promise that nothing will ever go wrong. Any online service can be unavailable for other reasons, which is why the plan above applies whatever software you use.

What we can say is how the engineers' [field app](/features/field-service) behaves without a signal. Jobs you have already opened, photos, defects, parts, signatures, job sheets and checklists save on the phone and send when the connection returns. A few actions need a connection: creating new jobs, attaching a checklist, creating a job sheet or a certificate draft, and deleting a defect. ## TL;DR

| Question | Short answer |
|---|---|
| First hour? | Confirm the cause, brief engineers, capture the schedule, split the calls |
| Engineers? | Paper job sheets, forms and test sheets in every van |
| Records? | Date, site, attendees, tests, defects and what the customer was told |
| Customers? | Short, honest updates; no promised fix times; no blame without confirmation |
| Export? | Customers and sites, jobs, quotes, invoices and certificate PDFs, stored securely |
| Supplier? | Ask about backups, export, support route and what offline means |

*This is general guidance, not legal advice. Your certification scheme, insurer and customer contracts may set additional requirements.*
